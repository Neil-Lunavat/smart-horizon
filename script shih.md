A GNSS receiver listens to satellites and measures how long each signal took to arrive. Four satellites are enough to solve for three position coordinates plus the receiver's own clock offset. The position you get is only as good as each satellite's own knowledge of where it was and what time it was. Both are broadcast, and both are slightly wrong. 1 nanosecond of satellite clock error is about 30 cm of position error. Roughly 30 satellites are above the horizon at any time. There are two orbit classes, GEO which is Geostationary, and MEO which is medium earth orbit. GEO sits over a fixed point on the equator. MEO is where constellations like GPS, Galileo, BeiDou operate at roughly half the altitude of GEOs. NavIC / IRNSS is entirely GEO and GSO, with no MEO satellites. The satellite error actually matters when we are precisely positioning, surveying, timing distribution or checking aviation integrity. The Smart Horizon Problem Statement has given us seven days of a satellite’s position error and clock error, and wants us to predict day 8 at arbitrary timestamps. Six CSV files, three training and three test. No satellite identifier, no constellation. The only external information given is the file label saying GEO or MEO. Position errors are in metres in Earth-fixed coordinates and clock error is a time offset. After cleaning the data from duplicates, analyzing gaps in coverage, checking range of errors and other statistical factors, we start our machine learning attempt. Six standard regressors give is RMS error in metres yet the best of them beats trivial baseline by only 7%. Trivial baseline is simply taking the average of the seven days we were given and using that one number for every moment of day 8. No model, no fitting, no effort. The reason for the plateau is not taking into account the satellite’s orbital phase, the constellation, the last upload from ground segment, none of which are in our dataset. Therefore we must take a different approach. Physics. A geosynchronous satellite repeats its geometry every sidereal day, not every solar day. That is 23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds. Over seven days the sidereal day and the solar day drift about 28 minutes apart, which is enough to break any fit that assumes 24 hours. A seven day window therefore contains 7.02 exact repeats. Nothing has to be searched for, it is given to us. Kepler's third law also fixes the geostationary radius at 42,164 km. MEO periods are fixed by constellation design. GPS is 11.97 hours, Galileo is 14.08 hours, BeiDou MEO is 12.89 hours. They differ by about 17%, and the file does not say which one we have. Now the coordinate frame, because this is where it gets interesting. The errors are given in ECEF, Earth centred Earth fixed. z points at the north pole along the spin axis, x lies in the equatorial plane through Greenwich, and y lies in the equatorial plane through 90 degrees east. Because the Earth spins about z, the x and y axes are dragged around once per sidereal day. z is untouched by the rotation. So one metre of error in x is not a fixed physical direction. It means something different twelve hours later. The satellite moves at its orbital period T. The frame we measure it in rotates at the sidereal period. What we actually observe is the product of the two rotations. And there is a school identity for that. Sine alpha cos beta is one half of sine of alpha minus beta plus sine of alpha plus beta. One tone becomes two. Which means x and y never appear at the orbital period. They appear at two beat periods, one over the absolute value of one over T plus or minus one over T sidereal. Only z, which the rotation never touches, keeps the orbital period. For GPS, x and y appear at 7.98 hours and 23.93 hours while z stays at 11.97. For Galileo, 8.86 and 34.19 against 14.08. For BeiDou, 8.38 and 27.92 against 12.89. That 23.93 hours for GPS is the sidereal day itself. It is the textbook fact that GPS ground tracks repeat daily, and it falls straight out of the algebra without anyone putting it there. That is our check that the derivation is correct. The consequence is that any model fitting a single rhythm to all three axes is guaranteed to be wrong on two of them. And GEO and MEO are the same equation at two settings. Set T equal to the sidereal day and the two beats collapse back onto the sidereal day. One model, not two. Then the actual test. We computed these periods from orbital mechanics, drew them on an empty periodogram, and only then ran the frequency analysis on the delivered files. MEO-1 and MEO-2 peak exactly on the predicted band. The GEO file does not peak at the sidereal day. It peaks near 12 hours, which no geostationary satellite can produce. That was the first sign that the GEO file is not what it claims to be. We wrote it down and moved on. It comes back later. The clock is a different kind of signal altogether. An atomic clock's error is a random walk from thermal noise and ageing, punctuated by step resets when the ground segment uploads a correction. Frequency noise integrates as the square root of elapsed time, and neither the random walk nor the resets is periodic. Their own data agrees. MEO_Train2 has 143 rows but only 70 distinct clock values, one of them repeating 17 times. That is a step process, not a smooth curve. So the model is forbidden from fitting rhythms to the clock channel. Giving it more freedom there makes results worse, because it fits noise and then extrapolates it. There is also a hard limit here that we should state honestly. A window's absolute offset is mathematically unrecoverable, because there is no absolute reference inside a window to measure it against. For the clock, where there is no oscillation, that offset is the entire signal. That is a limit of the problem, not of the method. Now the model itself. Three layers, and it carries no trained weights at all. One, the hypothesis space is fixed by orbital mechanics before any data is read. Two, the basis is chosen per window by held out validation. Three, the coefficients are fitted by ridge regression. In practice it cuts the seven days it is handed six and one. Day 7 is held out. Every admissible basis, plus flat level estimators, is fitted on days 1 to 6 and scored on day 7. The winner is refitted on all seven days and pushed into day 8. The error it made on the hidden day is what it reports as its 1 sigma uncertainty. The only model selection signal comes from the caller's own file. The candidate menu is seven level estimators, means, recent means, exponentially weighted means, plus harmonic bases at the physically admissible periods. For MEO the period is estimated per window by a Lomb-Scargle periodogram, which tolerates irregular sampling and gaps, and it is restricted to the 38,000 to 54,000 second corridor that physics allows. The two beat periods are then derived by formula, never searched. A divergence clamp stops any fitted curve running away outside the range it was fitted on. It costs about 65 milliseconds per window on a single CPU core. Dependencies are numpy and pandas. No training step, no network, no GPU, no weights file. Input is exactly the five supplied columns. Output is four predictions plus a 1 sigma uncertainty on each, on a 15 minute grid covering the 24 hours after the last input row, which is 96 rows. Arbitrary query timestamps are supported. Duplicates, gaps, unsorted rows and irregular sampling are handled automatically. And because it fits its coefficients inside whatever window it is handed, at call time, it remembers nothing between satellites. An unseen satellite is not a distribution shift for it. Three real files cannot prove any of that works, so we built a generator. Every term in it is a documented physical effect. A static offset, orbital harmonics at the derived beat periods, slow drift between ground segment uploads, measurement noise, and a clock built as a random walk with step resets. It is sampled at the delivered files' own irregular timestamps, gaps and coverage, so the test is as hard as the real data. Then the protocol. Generate a window, hide day 8, ask the model for it, compare against the planted truth. The model never sees the answer. 120 windows, 60 per orbit class, each one a different satellite with its own period, phase, drift and noise draw. The model's design was fixed on separate generated data before these windows were drawn, so it was never tuned to this test set. The results, in RMS metres. On GEO, doing nothing is 0.804, the 7 day average is 0.425, and the model is 0.173. That is 78% better. On MEO, doing nothing is 0.760, the average is 0.421, and the model is 0.373. That is 51% better. Doing nothing here means predicting that the error stays at zero for the whole of day 8. The model beats the 7 day average in all 60 GEO windows and in 78% of the 60 MEO windows. Against that average specifically, GEO is 59% better and MEO is 11% better. The MEO margin against the average is the weakest number in this project, and we are saying it out loud. We also stressed it. As measurement noise rises from 0.01 m to 0.40 m it holds about 51% improvement, and at 1.0 m, where the noise is as large as the entire signal, it still gives 34%. But corrupt one row in ten with a sign flip and performance drops from plus 51% to about minus 288%, far worse than doing nothing. Corrupt half the rows and it reaches about minus 700%. Remember that number. On the delivered data now. On MEO-1, doing nothing is 0.444 and the model is 0.190, which is 57% better. On MEO-2, doing nothing is 0.164 and the model is 0.133, which is 19% better. It also beats an oracle handed the answer key's own average. 0.190 against 0.211 on MEO-1, and 0.133 against 0.136 on MEO-2. Beating that oracle is only possible by tracking the shape within the day, which no constant value can do. You can see it. On MEO-1 z error the prediction rises and falls with the truth across the six scored hours, while the 7 day average sits flat at 0.02 m and misses every single point. On MEO-2 x error, which is a deliberately chosen noisy case, the prediction tracks the trend but not the scatter. Both are shown. Neither was picked after the fact. We also benchmarked 16 methods, fit on days 1 to 6 of the delivered files and scored on day 7, with no outside data. SMART-HORIZON is first at 0.278, the median is 0.386, the mean is 0.387, and it goes down from there to seasonal naive and last value at 0.563. First of sixteen, 28% ahead of the runner up. And then there is GEO, where nothing works. Every method lands within 0.02 m of the others at about 14.95 m. Our model, predict zero, the 7 day average, the last value. All the same. Hand a predictor the answer key's own average, which is outright cheating, and it improves on doing nothing by 0.3%. When a cheat cannot win, there is nothing in the file to predict. And that conclusion does not depend on trusting our model. So we went and looked at why. Consecutive readings in the GEO file are near exact opposites. The worst cosine similarity is minus 0.9994. All four channels flip sign together, and position error comes from orbital dynamics while clock error comes from an atomic oscillator. Those are unrelated mechanisms. They cannot reverse in lockstep. Across the 24 largest excursions the sign alternates 24 times out of 24. The odds of that by chance are about 1 in 8 million. We tried to repair it. If it were a sign convention bug the signs could be propagated back, but the magnitudes, which no sign flip can change, are themselves incoherent. There is no correct series hiding underneath. We tried to exploit it. An oracle handed the answer key and told to delete the 24 bad jumps becomes 88% more accurate, and its normality score gets worse. The artefact cannot be turned into an advantage. And the flip is not predictable from sample number, from clock time, or from the gap structure. So we proved it by construction instead. We took a clean generated GEO signal that our model predicts to 0.017 m, injected exactly this alternating sign pattern, and changed nothing else. Same physics, same noise. The prediction flattens and the error becomes 0.743 m. The only difference between those two runs is the alternating sign. That is what happened to their file. One more thing worth saying, about how this is scored. The competition scores normality of the residuals with a Shapiro-Wilk statistic W between 0 and 1, higher being better. It measures whether the errors look like random bell curve noise. It says nothing at all about whether the errors are large. Their own reference file does not match the test they name. Standard Shapiro-Wilk on it gives W of 0.9852 and p of 0.8262, while they state 0.9810 and 0.5840. Shapiro-Francia gives 0.9814 and 0.5838, which reproduces their value exactly. The MATLAB implementation they appear to have used switches from Shapiro-Wilk to Shapiro-Francia when kurtosis exceeds 3, so our scorer reproduces that behaviour and we grade ourselves the way they will. And W is scale invariant, so it grades the shape of the error distribution and not its size. Predict zero, predict the 7 day average and predict the last value all score 0.7708, identical to four decimal places, on all three files. An oracle that is 99% accurate scores 0.7708 as well. Pure random noise scores 0.88 to 0.98, near the top of the scale, while being 5 to 12 times less accurate. There is also a ceiling. A flawless model cannot score 1.0 at these sample sizes. On MEO-1 with 6 points, perfectly random errors land around 0.92 and we score 0.907, which is already at that ceiling and rejects nothing at the 5% level. At six test points the spread from luck alone is wider than the gap between serious teams. We produced the Q-Q plots they asked for. The MEO channels sit close to the line. The GEO clock channel, at 0.536, does not, which is the same artefact showing up again in the residuals. Finally, we did not want anyone to have to take our word for any of this. So we built a sandbox. It runs in any browser, with no install and no server, running the same algorithm in JavaScript with the day 8 prediction redrawn live. You can move measurement noise, data coverage, corruption level, signal amplitude, orbit class and the random seed. There are four presets, and each one is a claim about the data rather than about the model. A MEO satellite, which shows two rhythms in x and y, neither of them the orbital period. Almost no data, where 90% of the week is missing and it still solves it, because the rhythm was never learned from the data in the first place. Not a satellite, where the data is physically impossible and the model declines to fit. And nothing to find, where no rhythm exists, so averaging is correct and the model just averages. The last two are the strongest demonstrations we have, because in both of them the model refuses to fit rather than producing a confident wrong answer. That is also why we are not worried about generalisation. There is no stored training set for a new satellite, a new month or a new constellation to be inconsistent with. Moving to NavIC just means supplying its orbital periods, GEO and inclined GSO, and the same closed form beat frequencies follow. Nothing here is tuned to GPS. And if the evaluation set turns out to be GEO like, every team's score will be noise. We did not find a pattern in this data. We worked out which patterns were allowed to exist, and let the file choose between them.
